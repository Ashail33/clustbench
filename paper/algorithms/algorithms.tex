\begin{abstract}
We report twenty-three rounds of algorithm development carried out inside
Clustbench~\cite{clustbenchharness}, a benchmark harness that re-ranks every
candidate against a registry of clustering algorithms on every run. The
algorithms were not designed up front; each round started from a failure mode
the previous round's benchmark exposed. We contribute
(i) a family of exponential-family mixture models whose component
log-densities are linear in a shared basis, with random Fourier (FMM), graph
Laplacian (LMM) and autoencoder (AMM) bases, plus multi-basis and
transform-feature variants;
(ii) a physics-inspired method (MRIC);
(iii) five targeted one-line fixes of classical methods;
(iv) three composed pipelines, each iterated through a v1 $\to$ v2 $\to$ v3
cycle;
(v) a graph-community method, \code{louvain\_knn};
(vi) ten learned algorithm selectors (\emph{routers});
(vii) four machine-proposed algorithms;
(viii) \code{rl\_pipeline}, a behaviour-cloned policy that clusters by
chaining eight primitive operators; and
(ix) graph clustering at linear cost: approximate-graph variants of LMM and
spectral clustering, and \emph{micro-cluster graph} methods (MCG) that build
the graph on about a thousand $k$-means micro-clusters linked by
competitive Hebbian edges.
All results come from a benchmark run with validity controls (five seeds,
post-hoc tests, real-data provenance checks, timing of the algorithm call
alone, run isolation). Graph-based methods are the strongest single
algorithms in-distribution: \code{lmm\_mcg\_dual}, LMM, \code{lmm\_mcg} and
spectral clustering have mean ARI 0.763--0.769 among 61 algorithms. They
cluster by connectivity rather than by distance to a centre, and on manifolds
their eigenvectors are cosine modes along the data. The MCG methods keep
that accuracy at linear cost. \code{lmm\_mcg\_dual} is
statistically tied with LMM in-distribution (0.769). On a held-out split the
four MCG variants have the four highest means (0.890--0.925 against 0.858
for LMM), though none of the differences survives multiple-comparison
correction. At $n = 100{,}000$, \code{mcg\_cf} matches LMM's accuracy 54
times faster. The best routers beat every single algorithm by about 0.05
ARI in-distribution. Retrained without the dataset family under test, they
lose 0.03--0.07 and are statistically tied with LMM. On curved shapes they
choose centroid methods and score 0.31--0.35, against 0.74--0.86 for graph
methods. Targeted fixes frequently traded the diagnosed failure for a new
one, and several of our own earlier results did not reproduce.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
No clustering algorithm is best on all data~\cite{kleinberg2002impossibility,wolpert1997nfl},
and the classical families fail in characteristic ways. Centroid methods
cannot represent non-convex clusters. Mean-based updates are dragged by
outliers. Density methods depend on a scale parameter. Graph methods pay
quadratic eigendecomposition costs. A companion paper~\cite{clustbenchharness}
describes Clustbench, the harness used here. It runs a registry of algorithms
on a grid of synthetic, real, time-series, graph and adversarial datasets,
records quality and cost, and aggregates by average rank and the Friedman
test~\cite{demsar2006statistical}.

This paper reports what we built with it. The process was empirical
iteration: run the benchmark, name the narrowest failure mode visible in the
per-shape and per-outlier breakdowns, and build the smallest change that
addresses it. Section~\ref{sec:method} makes this loop explicit.
Section~\ref{sec:algos} describes the algorithms. Section~\ref{sec:results}
evaluates them in-distribution, on an out-of-distribution split, at
$n = 100{,}000$, and on every dataset generator in the harness. It also
explains why graph-based methods win on curved data.
Section~\ref{sec:lessons} states the lessons, several of them negative.

\section{Methodology: empirical iteration}\label{sec:method}

The benchmark's per-algorithm analysis reduced almost every weakness to one of
six failure modes: (F1) outlier sensitivity of mean-based updates; (F2)
non-convex shape failure of Voronoi-cell methods; (F3) super-linear scaling;
(F4) hyperparameter brittleness; (F5) local-minimum search; and (F6) a wrong
structural assumption (e.g.\ subspace clustering on isotropic blobs). Each
failure mode has a short menu of known remedies. That observation motivates a
five-stage loop:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{v1, synthesis.} Compose the mechanisms that already won on each
  axis: $k$-means EM for convex blobs, a graph-Laplacian embedding for
  non-convex shape, posterior weighting for outliers, single-pass trees for
  scale, data-adaptive parameters for robustness, and diverse ensembles.
  \item \textbf{Diagnose.} Benchmark v1 and name its \emph{specific} new
  failure, e.g.\ ``ARI 0.00 on circles because a full-covariance GMM absorbs
  both rings into one Gaussian.''
  \item \textbf{v2, targeted fix.} Change only the mechanism the diagnosis
  names.
  \item \textbf{v3, dispatch.} If v1 and v2 win on different regimes, route
  between them on a cheap data signature.
  \item \textbf{Learned dispatch.} Replace hand-coded routing with a model
  trained on the benchmark's own (task fingerprint, algorithm, ARI) table.
\end{enumerate}
The loop stops when per-iteration lift falls within noise, when the router
concentrates on one choice, or when no regime in the grid can show a lift. In
that last case the right move is to enlarge the benchmark, not the algorithm.

\section{Algorithms}\label{sec:algos}

\subsection{Exponential-family basis mixtures: FMM, LMM, AMM}\label{sec:basis}
GMMs~\cite{dempster1977maximum} assume Gaussian components. We replace each
component with an exponential family whose sufficient statistics are a shared,
fixed basis $\phi:\R^d\to\R^M$:
\[
  p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,p_k(x;\alpha_k),\qquad
  p_k(x;\alpha_k) \propto \exp\!\big(\alpha_k^\top\phi(x)\big).
\]
The log-partition function is intractable for a general basis. We sidestep it
by \emph{self-normalising over the data}:
\[
  \log p_k(x_i) = \alpha_k^\top\phi(x_i) - \log\sum_{j=1}^{n}\exp\!\big(\alpha_k^\top\phi(x_j)\big),
\]
which turns $p_k$ into a discrete distribution over the observed points. The
E-step computes responsibilities $\gamma_{ik}$ as usual. The M-step is
concave in each $\alpha_k$ and is solved with one damped Newton step,
\[
  \big(\mathrm{Cov}_{q_k}[\phi] + \lambda I\big)\,\Delta\alpha_k
  = \tfrac{1}{N_k}\textstyle\sum_i\gamma_{ik}\phi(x_i) - \mathbb{E}_{q_k}[\phi] - \lambda\alpha_k,
\]
where $q_k$ is the softmax of $\alpha_k^\top\phi(x_j)$ over the data. The
Hessian is a covariance matrix, so EM converges in a handful of outer
iterations, and every iteration is logged as a trajectory step. The three
members differ only in $\phi$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{FMM} uses random Fourier features~\cite{rahimi2007random},
  $\phi(x) = [\cos\omega_m^\top x, \sin\omega_m^\top x]_{m=1}^{M}$. The
  frequencies are drawn from a mixture of bandwidths centred on the inverse
  median pairwise distance, so one model resolves both fine and coarse
  structure.
  \item \textbf{LMM} uses the bottom eigenvectors $\psi$ of the normalised
  $k$NN-graph Laplacian~\cite{vonluxburg2007tutorial}, i.e.\ the spectral
  embedding, weighted by a per-cluster heat kernel
  $W_m(\tau_k) = e^{-\tau_k\lambda_m/2}$. Here $\lambda_m$ is the $m$-th
  Laplacian eigenvalue, so $\tau_k$ is a learned graph-diffusion bandwidth:
  small $\tau_k$ keeps neighbourhood detail, large $\tau_k$ averages over a
  connected component. LMM can be read as spectral clustering with soft,
  EM-trained assignments in place of a final $k$-means.
  \item \textbf{AMM} uses the bottleneck activations of a shallow autoencoder.
  It is intended for high-dimensional sparse inputs, where $k$NN distances
  lose meaning.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The name ``Laplacian mixture model'' is taken.}
  Korenblum~\cite{korenblum2018laplacian} combines Laplacian eigenspaces with
  finite mixture modelling to obtain fuzzy partitions. That model is fitted
  by globally optimising a linear transformation of the eigenvectors (a
  linearly constrained concave quadratic programme), with optimal-recovery
  guarantees for a class of cluster graphs. Our LMM shares the
  Laplacian-eigenvector basis and soft assignments. It differs in fitting
  per-cluster exponential-family log-densities on that basis by EM, with
  self-normalisation over the data and a learned per-cluster heat-kernel
  diffusion time. We keep the abbreviation for continuity with the code, but
  the method should be read as a variant within that line of work, not a new
  model class.
  \item \textbf{FMM's ingredients are known.} EM for mixtures of exponential
  families is classical~\cite{banerjee2005clustering}. Exponential families
  over kernel or random-feature bases are studied as density
  estimators~\cite{sriperumbudur2017density}, typically fitted by score
  matching rather than by maximum likelihood, because of the partition
  function. Random Fourier features have been used for kernel
  clustering~\cite{chitta2012efficient}. We did not find this exact
  combination: a mixture whose components are random-Fourier exponential
  families with the log-partition self-normalised over the sample, fitted by
  Newton-step EM. Our search was limited, though, and we make no strong novelty
  claim.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Combined bases and transform features (round 17)}\label{sec:transforms}
Two questions followed from the basis mixtures: does combining bases help,
and can classical integral transforms replace them?
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{TBMM} concatenates the graph-Laplacian, random-Fourier and
  autoencoder bases into one $\phi$, warm-started from a spectral solution.
  \code{tbmm\_fl} drops the autoencoder block.
  \item \textbf{FLHMM} clusters on three per-point transform features:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item random Fourier features (an empirical characteristic function);
    \item a Laplace transform of each point's distance distribution,
    $\tfrac1n\sum_j e^{-s\,d_{ij}}$ on a log-grid of $s$;
    \item the envelope and instantaneous phase of the analytic (Hilbert)
    signal of the density projected on random directions.
  \end{itemize}
  The blocks are scaled to equal variance and concatenated, with a mixture
  head (\code{flhmm}) or a $k$-means head (\code{flhmm\_km}).
  \item \textbf{FLH-cycle} keeps one mixture per transform view and shares
  only the soft assignments. Each step runs one view's M-step, then an
  E-step, cycling Fourier $\to$ Laplace $\to$ Hilbert. This is co-EM
  \cite{nigam2000analyzing,bickel2004multiview} with transforms as views.
  \code{flh\_cycle\_product} instead computes each E-step from all views'
  likelihoods (block-coordinate EM on the product of experts), with a 50\%
  hand-off.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{MRIC: magnetic-resonance-inspired clustering}
MRI separates tissues because nuclei in different chemical environments relax
at different rates. MRIC treats each point as a spin and its $k$NN
neighbourhood as its environment. The first principal axis plays the static
field $B_0$. Local density (inverse mean $k$NN distance) and local anisotropy
(from the eigenvalues of the local covariance) map to per-point $T_1/T_2$
relaxation times. A simulated free-induction-decay signal is sampled into a
per-point signature, and $k$-means clusters the signatures.

\subsection{Targeted fixes of classical methods}
Each fix changes one mechanism named by the diagnosis:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{dbscan\_auto}: $\varepsilon$ set from the knee of the
  $k$-distance plot~\cite{ester1996density} (F4).
  \item \code{meanshift\_robust}: bandwidth estimated on a trimmed sample
  (F1/F4).
  \item \code{clarans\_pp}: $k$-means++ medoid seeding~\cite{arthur2007kmeanspp}
  (F5).
  \item \code{pwcc\_diverse}: PWCC~\cite{alguliyev2020weighted} with
  heterogeneous bases $\{k\text{-means}, \text{spectral}, \text{GMM}\}$ in
  place of three $k$-means variants (F2).
  \item \code{kmeans\_trimmed}: drop the farthest 10\% of members from each
  centroid update (F1).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Composed pipelines and their v1 $\to$ v3 lineages}{Composed pipelines and their v1 to v3 lineages}}\label{sec:pipelines}

\paragraph{RAPID (Region-Adaptive Partitioning with Iterative Density).}
\emph{v1} partitions the data into density-connected regions with
auto-$\varepsilon$ DBSCAN. It computes a convexity signature per region and
sends convex regions to $k$-means++ and non-convex regions to spectral
clustering, then reassigns DBSCAN noise by 1-NN. Diagnosis: the $k$-distance
knee is contaminated by the outliers it should ignore (outlier $\Delta$ARI
$-0.145$). \emph{v2} adds a stage-0 LOF~\cite{breunig2000lof} pre-filter that
holds out the top 10\% of LOF scores. \emph{v3} runs the v2 path only when the
estimated LOF outlier fraction exceeds 5\%.

\paragraph{AURA (Adaptive Unified Robust Algorithm).}
\emph{v1} computes a Nyström-approximated~\cite{williams2001nystrom,fowlkes2004spectral}
normalised-Laplacian embedding $Y$ and fits a full-covariance GMM on it,
aiming for spectral shape-handling plus GMM outlier robustness. Diagnosis:
ARI 0.00 on circles, because the GMM merges both rings into one elongated
component and the eigen-columns differ in scale by $\sim\!20\times$.
\emph{v2} z-scores $Y$ column-wise and runs $k$-means. \emph{v3} counts the
informative embedding axes,
$r = |\{j : \mathrm{sd}(Y_{:j}) \ge 0.1\max_{j'}\mathrm{sd}(Y_{:j'})\}|$,
and routes $r \ge 2$ to the v2 path (z-scored $k$-means) and $r < 2$ to the v1
path (raw GMM). Moons have one informative axis and circles two, so each
version runs on its own regime.

\paragraph{META-CLUSTERER.}
\emph{v1} computes four fingerprints (PCA effective dimension, Laplacian
eigengap, LOF outlier fraction, $k$NN density skew) and dispatches with
hand-written rules. Diagnosis: the eigengap rule never fires on clean circles,
whose gap is \emph{large}. \emph{v2} replaces the eigengap with a convexity
ratio of the $k$-means partition and the $k$NN-graph
modularity~\cite{newman2006modularity} of that partition. It also adds a
silhouette probe that fits three candidates on a 20\% subsample when a rule
fires with low margin. \emph{v3} runs both routers and probes only when they
disagree.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\code{louvain\_knn}}{louvain\_knn}: a graph-native method for a coverage gap}
Enlarging the benchmark to graph-node features exposed Zachary's karate
club~\cite{zachary1977information} as a regime where every algorithm scored
below 0.43 ARI. With 34 nodes and 16 features, re-embedding the features
drowns the Fiedler signal. \code{louvain\_knn} builds a symmetrised $k$NN
graph on the features and optimises modularity directly with
Louvain~\cite{blondel2008fast}. It then reconciles the discovered community
count with the requested $k$: it merges the smallest community into its most
densely connected neighbour, or splits the largest community by spectral
bipartition. It falls back to a self-contained NumPy Louvain implementation,
and then to spectral clustering, if dependencies are missing.

\subsection{Learned routers}\label{sec:routers}
A router computes a fingerprint of the input, chooses an algorithm from the
registry, and runs it. It is trained on the benchmark's own results: every
historical task is regenerated from its specification, fingerprinted, and
joined with every algorithm's ARI on it. Training happens at import time, so
a router retrains whenever the results file changes. Table~\ref{tab:routers}
summarises the ten generations.

prevent recursion. For honest in-distribution evaluation they drop an exact
fingerprint self-match (task-level leave-one-out).

\subsection{Machine-proposed algorithms}
\paragraph{Card mutator.} Clustbench encodes every algorithm as a card listing
its mechanisms, inductive biases and complexity~\cite{clustbenchharness}. A
mutator searches card space with swap, add-bias, crossover and stack
operators and scores candidates by a bias-matching ARI upper bound. Its top
proposal, \code{mutant\_kmeans\_meta}, crosses $k$-means with
META-CLUSTERER-v2. It keeps the $k$-means partition when a convexity probe and
a silhouette check both pass, and otherwise hands off to the META-v2 router.

\paragraph{Multi-agent discovery (round 14).} LLM agents running in parallel
proposed algorithms, ordinary datasets and adversarial datasets. Separate
agents implemented, integrated and benchmarked them. Three algorithms came out
of this round:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \code{density\_peaks\_bridge}: density peaks~\cite{rodriguez2014clustering}
  plus one round of normalised-Laplacian label
  spreading~\cite{zhou2004learning}, which reassigns borderline halo points and
  keeps true outliers.
  \item \code{bico\_stream}: a BICO-style~\cite{fichtenberger2013bico} streaming
  coreset with merge-and-reduce~\cite{harpeled2004coresets}, followed by
  weighted $k$-means++ on the coreset.
  \item \code{isomap\_bgmm}: an Isomap~\cite{tenenbaum2000global} geodesic
  embedding followed by a Dirichlet-process Bayesian GMM~\cite{blei2006variational}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Trident (round 15).} Designed from the round-14 winners table,
Trident runs one cheap probe per winning family (centroid: $k$-means;
mixture: diagonal GMM; graph: \code{louvain\_knn}). It gates on silhouette and
pairwise-ARI agreement between the probes, and escalates to \code{isomap\_bgmm}
on a heavy-tail signature or to AURA-v3 on density variation. Selection is
built into the algorithm rather than delegated to an external router.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\code{rl\_pipeline}}{rl\_pipeline}: clustering as sequential decision-making}\label{sec:rl}
Clustbench's trajectory layer records iterative algorithms as
state--action--cost sequences~\cite{clustbenchharness}. We ask whether a
policy can \emph{compose} an algorithm from primitives. The environment has
eight actions drawn from the registry's dominant mechanisms:
\code{kpp\_init}, \code{assign\_to\_centers}, \code{update\_centers}
($k$-means); \code{medoid\_swap} (CLARANS); \code{ward\_merge} (Ward);
\code{density\_partition} (DBSCAN); \code{eigen\_embed} (spectral); and
\code{outlier\_trim}. The state is a 20-dimensional vector: $n$, $d$, $k$,
cost, silhouette, plateau signals, outlier mask statistics and centroid
statistics. The reward is $\mathrm{clip}(-\Delta c_t / c_0)$.

Hand-written action sequences reproduce their source algorithms: a $k$-means
sequence reaches ARI 1.0 on blobs, and the spectral sequence
\code{kpp}$\to$\code{eigen\_embed}$\to$\code{assign}$\to$\code{update} reaches
1.0 on circles. For behaviour cloning~\cite{pomerleau1989alvinn}, we drove
$k$-means, CLARANS, Ward, spectral clustering and DBSCAN through the
environment, which produced 50 episodes and 419 (state, action, reward) rows.
We trained an MLP policy (cross-entropy, with count$^{-1/4}$ class
re-weighting) and an MLP value head (discounted return, $\gamma{=}0.95$) on an
80/20 episode split. At inference the agent runs 16 stochastic rollouts, and a
tiered selector keeps the best non-embedding rollout with silhouette $\ge
0.40$, or else the best embedding rollout.

\subsection{Graph clustering without the point graph (rounds 19--23)}\label{sec:mcg}
LMM and spectral clustering spend almost all their time on the $k$NN graph
over all $n$ points. Building it exactly costs $O(n^2 d)$: 8.7\,s of LMM's
11.2\,s at $n = 50{,}000$, $d = 20$. Its eigenvectors then cost
$O(n \cdot \text{iterations})$, and on manifold-shaped data an
unpreconditioned LOBPCG needs 184 iterations (16.6\,s at $n = 10^5$). We
attacked both costs.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Approximate graph, preconditioned eigensolver}
  (\code{lmm\_fast}, \code{spectral\_fast}). The graph is built with
  NN-descent~\cite{dong2011efficient} once $n \ge 10^4$ and $d > 10$. It is
  12$\times$ faster than exact search at $n = 2\cdot10^5$ and recovers 95--98\%
  of the true neighbours at $d = 20$. LOBPCG~\cite{knyazev2001toward} is
  preconditioned with plain-aggregation algebraic multigrid
  (PyAMG~\cite{bell2022pyamg}), which needs about six iterations on the same
  data.
  \item \textbf{MCG: a graph on micro-clusters} (\code{mcg}). Fit mini-batch
  $k$-means with $m = \min(1000, n/10)$ centres on a subsample. Each point
  votes for an edge between its nearest and second-nearest centre:
  competitive Hebbian learning~\cite{martinetz1994topology}, whose edge counts
  are the CONN similarity of Ta\c{s}demir and Mer\'enyi~\cite{tasdemir2009exploiting}.
  Edges therefore exist only where data lies \emph{between} two centres.
  Counts are weighted by $\exp(-\|c_i-c_j\|^2/2\sigma^2)$, and spectral
  clustering runs on the $m \times m$ graph; each point takes its centre's
  label. The cost is $O(nmd)$ plus a fixed $O(m^3)$. Spectral clustering on
  $k$-means centroids is KASP~\cite{yan2009fast}, which links centroids by
  their positions alone. Spectral clustering on a $k$NN graph of the same
  centroids scores 0.27 on rings at $n = 2\cdot10^4$, where the point-voted
  edges score 0.97.
  \item \textbf{Tiers and CF triples} (\code{mcg\_cf}). Micro-clusters are
  built coarse-to-fine: a $k$-means with about $2\sqrt m$ centres, then local
  splits in proportion to each coarse cluster's size, as in BIRCH's CF
  tree~\cite{zhang1996birch}. Each edge's bandwidth then comes from the two
  micro-clusters' own spread, read from their clustering-feature triples
  $(N, \mathrm{LS}, \mathrm{SS})$ as $R^2 = \mathrm{SS}/N - \|\mathrm{LS}/N\|^2$:
  \[
    w_{ij} = \mathrm{count}_{ij}\,
    \exp\!\big(-\|c_i-c_j\|^2 / \gamma(R_i^2+R_j^2)\big),
  \]
  a locally scaled affinity in the sense of self-tuning spectral
  clustering~\cite{zelnikmanor2004self}. The parts fix opposite failures:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item tiers alone fix small real data (breast cancer
    $0.24 \to 0.45$) but break extreme outliers ($0.56 \to 0.27$);
    \item CF weights alone fix outliers (0.81) but not small data.
  \end{itemize}
  Multi-resolution anchor graphs~\cite{liu2010large,huang2020ultra} are the
  closest prior work.
  \item \textbf{LMM on the micro-cluster basis} (\code{lmm\_mcg}). The bottom
  eigenvectors of the micro-cluster graph replace LMM's point-graph basis. Each
  point takes its nearest micro-cluster's row, and LMM's EM then runs on the
  points.
  \item \textbf{A coarse-grained Laplacian for manifolds}
  (\code{lmm\_mcg\_dual}). The Gaussian edge weights that sharpen cuts across
  gaps also bend the geometry of smooth, gap-free sheets
  (\S\ref{sec:why}). With plain edge counts (a boundary-flux estimate) and
  the mass-weighted eigenproblem $(D - W)v = \lambda\,\mathrm{diag}(N)\,v$,
  the micro-cluster Laplacian is a finite-volume coarse-graining of the point
  graph's. It recovers LMM's eigenvectors. Each point's basis is then blended
  linearly between its two nearest micro-clusters. \code{lmm\_mcg\_dual}
  gives EM both bases side by side ($2k$ columns).
\end{itemize}
We also tried two other second stages on the same micro-cluster graph, and
both lost:
\begin{itemize}
  \item SVD of the soft point-to-micro-cluster bipartite
  graph~\cite{liu2010large}: 0.59--0.70 in-distribution;
  \item support vector clustering~\cite{benhur2001support} on the
  count-weighted micro-clusters, with the kernel width bisected to give $k$
  components: 0.66--0.69.
\end{itemize}
The first blurs boundaries between touching clusters. The second cannot
separate clusters joined by a bridge.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

Unless stated otherwise, numbers come from the controlled benchmark
rerun~\cite{clustbenchharness}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item an in-distribution split of 61 algorithms on 140 tasks (28 task
  variants from 20 dataset families, seeds 1--5, $n \le 1000$);
  \item a leave-one-dataset-family-out (LOFO) evaluation of the routers on
  the same tasks;
  \item an out-of-distribution (OOD) split of 62 algorithms on 35 tasks with
  unseen $(n,d,k)$, outlier extremities and seeds 100--104;
  \item a large-$n$ sweep up to $n = 50{,}000$, a big-data sweep up to
  $n = 200{,}000$, and graph clustering at $n = 100{,}000$ on eleven
  families;
  \item an every-dataset run of 63 algorithms on 180 tasks from all 36
  dataset generators.
\end{itemize}
The MCG settings (number of micro-clusters, edge weighting, $\gamma$, hard
assignment) were chosen on the OOD split and a 20k-point development suite,
with the in-distribution tasks held out. The \code{lmm\_mcg\_dual} basis was
developed on the curved shapes of the every-dataset run, so its numbers there
are not held out.
Only \code{fit\_predict} is timed (median of three runs, one BLAS thread).
Lineage tables (Tables~\ref{tab:fixes}--\ref{tab:lineage}) come from the
round in which each version was introduced, on the 16-task demonstration grid
and before the controls, and are marked as such.

\subsection{Why graph methods win on curved data}\label{sec:why}
$k$-means is good on blobs because it assigns points by straight-line
distance. LMM and spectral clustering effectively assign points by distance
\emph{along the data}. The $k$NN graph only links nearby points, and the
graph Laplacian's bottom eigenvectors give coordinates in which straight-line
distance approximates diffusion distance on the
graph~\cite{belkin2003laplacian,coifman2006diffusion}. Clustering in those
coordinates groups points by connectivity.

The S-curve and Swiss roll make this concrete. Their true clusters are
equal-mass slices of the curve parameter $t$, with no gaps between slices. On
both, LMM's first three non-trivial eigenvectors are the cosine modes
$\cos(m\pi s)$ of position $s$ along the curve:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $|r| = 1.00, 1.00, 0.99$ on the S-curve;
  \item $|r| = 0.99, 0.97, 0.94$ on the Swiss roll;
  \item correlation with the sheet's height below 0.05 on both.
\end{itemize}
The embedding unrolls the sheet (Figure~\ref{fig:manifold}, left). Graph
distance tracks distance along the curve with rank correlation 0.97--0.99.
Straight-line distance tracks it with 0.88 on the gently bent S-curve but
only 0.25 on the Swiss roll, whose layers are close in 3-D. So $k$-means
scores 0.91 on the S-curve but 0.21 on the Swiss roll, while LMM scores 0.80
and 0.73. Clustering on the true $t$ itself scores 0.84 (the truth slices by
mass, $k$-means on $t$ by width), so LMM is near the ceiling.

The same analysis explains a weakness of \code{lmm\_mcg}. Its Gaussian
edge weights distort these modes on the Swiss roll ($|r| = 0.89, 0.62, 0.22$;
Figure~\ref{fig:manifold}, middle). The coarse-grained count Laplacian of
\S\ref{sec:mcg} recovers them ($|r| = 1.00, 0.97, 0.92$; right) at the
micro-cluster cost. That basis gives 0.82 and 0.73 on the S-curve and Swiss
roll, level with LMM, but it cuts gaps less sharply: 0.863 OOD against
0.925 for the CF basis. The dual basis keeps most of both.

\subsection{Graph accuracy at linear cost}\label{sec:fast}
Figure~\ref{fig:largen} places every graph method at $n = 100{,}000$, on
eleven families at $d = 20$ with two seeds.
\begin{itemize}
  \item LMM scores 0.837 in 40\,s; exact spectral clustering is capped at
  $2\cdot10^4$.
  \item The approximate-graph variants keep the accuracy at a fifth of the
  time: \code{lmm\_fast} 0.844 in 8.5\,s and \code{spectral\_fast} 0.832 in
  8.9\,s. Without the AMG preconditioner they had scored 0.836 and 0.776,
  because plain LOBPCG did not converge on rings.
  \item The MCG family is fastest and as accurate: \code{mcg\_cf} 0.859 in
  0.74\,s (54$\times$ faster than LMM), \code{mcg} 0.853 in 1.5\,s,
  \code{lmm\_mcg} 0.858 in 2.0\,s and \code{lmm\_mcg\_dual} 0.858 in
  3.3\,s.
  \item Centroid methods are faster still but score about 0.56 on these
  families.
\end{itemize}
On the big-data axes \code{mcg\_cf}'s wall time grows with exponents 0.32,
0.23 and 0.13 in $n$, $d$ and $k$. It finishes $n = 2\cdot10^5$ in 1.1\,s and
the $10^5 \times 100 \times 50$ corner in 1.3\,s, both at ARI 1.00; LMM needs
102\,s for the corner. The EM-based variants inherit LMM's Newton step,
which allocates a $(k, M, n)$ array for a basis of size $M$: \code{lmm\_mcg}
takes 21\,s at $k = 50$, and \code{lmm\_mcg\_dual} ($M = 2k$) runs out of
memory there, as LMM does at $k = 200$.

\subsection{Routers: in-distribution gains that do not transfer}
Figure~\ref{fig:lineage} plots every router generation on three evaluations.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{In-distribution, routers hold the top seven ranks.} The best
  beats LMM by 0.051 ARI (paired Wilcoxon, Holm $p = 2\times10^{-4}$).
  \item \textbf{Within the lineage, v3 beats v1} (Holm $p = 0.003$). v2's
  probe features and v3's switch from rank voting to per-algorithm ARI
  regression each added roughly 0.02--0.03 ARI when introduced.
  \item \textbf{Architectural changes helped only when they added a new
  signal.} v4 used the same features with a different model and overrode v3
  on 51 of 52 tasks without improving, so the v5 stacker over $\{$v3, v4$\}$
  degenerated to always-v3. v6's probe signal is different in kind and won
  exactly one task v3 missed (inverse-PCA, $0.00 \to 0.38$). That was enough
  for the v7 stacker over $\{$v3, v6$\}$ to take rank 1 in round 8.
  \item \textbf{Silhouette is a biased probe.} As a probe metric it favours
  compact wrong answers on non-convex data, and adding a 20\% cost term (v6c)
  dethroned spectral clustering on close calls where it was correct.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Leave one family out.} Retraining each router without the family
under test removes the leakage of sibling seeds.
\begin{itemize}
  \item v3, v5 and v7 fall from 0.815 to 0.745, v1 from 0.788 to 0.728, and
  v8 from 0.808 to 0.748.
  \item v4, whose gradient-boosted regressors interpolate more smoothly, is the
  most robust: 0.814 to 0.784.
  \item The losses concentrate where a family's winner is unusual. With moons
  held out, v3 never learns to send moons-like data to \code{louvain\_knn} and
  loses 0.70 ARI there; the karate graph costs 0.32.
  \item Paired against LMM and spectral clustering on the same 140 tasks, no
  LOFO router differs significantly (Holm $p \ge 0.07$; mean differences
  from $-0.037$ to $+0.020$).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Unseen configurations.} On the OOD split, v3, v5 and v7 (0.845)
are level with spectral clustering (0.860), LMM (0.858) and
\code{louvain\_knn} (0.846), and behind the four MCG variants (0.890--0.925);
none of these differences survives Holm correction. Typical remaining failures are inverse-PCA at $d{=}150$ (v3 0.72
against 0.85 for Ward) and outlier extremity 10 (v3 0.74 against 0.92 for
\code{rl\_pipeline}). v8's richer fingerprint and OOD-triggered ensemble
again did not help (0.800).

An earlier version of this paper reported that v3--v7 lost 0.11--0.13 ARI on
the OOD split and that v1 generalised best. That result came from routers
trained on a narrower grid (three seeds, no inverse-PCA, synthetic stand-ins
for the UCR series) and does not reproduce. The robust conclusion is the LOFO
one: a router's advantage comes from having seen a family's winner.

\paragraph{Self-extension (development history, before the controls).} Routers
retrain from the results file, so adding \code{louvain\_knn} and re-running

fingerprint router to start dispatching to it on the karate graph. This

other side of the same mechanism: a router can only dispatch to a winner it
has seen.

\subsection{Machine-proposed algorithms}
The card mutator predicted 0.926 ARI for \code{mutant\_kmeans\_meta}; it
measured 0.747 at introduction and 0.719 in the controlled benchmark (rank 18).
It beats its parent \code{kmeans\_trimmed} (0.654) by 0.065, so the hybrid is
a real improvement, but the predicted magnitude was not. Bias matching can
order architectures but cannot calibrate them.

Of the round-14 proposals, the two adversarial datasets did what they were
designed to do: routers trailed non-routers by 0.036 and 0.068 ARI on them.
Of the three new algorithms, none reached the top 20.

Trident (rank 17, 0.725) shows the limit of silhouette gating. On moons it
scored 0.262 even though its own \code{louvain\_knn} probe scored 0.997,
because a compact wrong split can have a higher silhouette than a correct
non-convex one.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{\code{rl\_pipeline}}{rl\_pipeline}}
The behaviour-cloned policy reached 0.59 validation accuracy at predicting the
next action. Chance is 0.125 and the majority-class baseline is 0.41. In the
original 300-point smoke test it scored ARI 1.00 on blobs and 1.00 on
circles, where $k$-means scores $-0.003$. On circles it chose
\code{eigen\_embed} early: from imitation alone, it learned to switch to the
spectral embedding when the cost stops falling. On moons it scored 0.26,
because a $k$-means-like rollout won the silhouette tie-break.

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} The traces and checkpoint lived in a git-ignored
directory, which is why the agent produced no rows in round 14. The agent now
rebuilds both deterministically when they are missing. The rebuilt traces
match the originals exactly: 419 rows, 50 episodes, and the same action
counts. A checkpoint retrained on them (PyTorch 2.14) reproduces blobs (1.00)
and moons (0.30), but scores \emph{0.16} on the circles smoke test, not 1.00.
That headline result therefore depended on one lucky training run.

\paragraph{Controlled rerun.} The agent ranks 46th of 61 in-distribution (mean
ARI 0.604) and is the slowest method (median 2.4\,s per task), with a bimodal
profile: 0.989 on the SBM graph and 0.907 on anisotropic blobs, but $-0.001$
on circles and 0.27 on moons at $n{=}500$. It does better OOD (rank 23,
0.764), where it is the best algorithm on outlier extremity 10 (0.92).
\begin{itemize}
  \item At $n{=}500$ the policy still fires \code{eigen\_embed}, but every
  rollout then stalls on \code{update\_centers} no-ops. 419 imitation rows
  cannot teach the coordinated sequence that completes a spectral partition.
  \item This is the behaviour-cloning ceiling. Imitation of per-step
  decisions does not value a move by where the episode ends, and compounding
  errors off the demonstrator distribution are
  expected~\cite{ross2011reduction}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Lessons}\label{sec:lessons}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Targeted fixes often open new failure modes.} Two of the five
  one-line fixes (\code{dbscan\_auto}, \code{clarans\_pp}) and two of the three
  pipeline v2s (AURA, RAPID) traded the diagnosed failure for a new one. The
  clean pipeline win was META v2, where a brittle rule was strictly replaced.
  \item \textbf{Dispatch pays only when versions are complementary on
  detectable regimes.} AURA-v3 (+0.142) succeeded because effective rank
  separates moons from circles. RAPID-v3 could not help because v2 never won.
  META-v3 lost because its probe was noisier than the v2 it refined.
  \item \textbf{Stacking needs architectural diversity.} Re-fitting the same
  features with a different model (v4) added nothing. A different signal (v6's
  probe) added a real, if small, gain.
  \item \textbf{Learned selection does not transfer to unseen families.} Routers
  beat every single algorithm in-distribution, but with the test family held
  out they are statistically tied with LMM and spectral clustering. Only a
  family-level split shows this; a seed-level or configuration-level hold-out
  does not. On real datasets missing from their training table they tie the
  best single methods (0.30), and on curved shapes they pick centroid methods
  (0.31--0.35). Outside the benchmark's families, graph-based methods (the
  MCG family, LMM, spectral clustering, \code{louvain\_knn}) are as good a
  default and much cheaper.
  \item \textbf{Grow the registry, not the router.} The biggest router gains
  came from adding algorithms and data, not from router redesigns.
  \item \textbf{Internal-validity gates are shape-biased.} Silhouette-based
  probes (v6, META-v3, Trident) repeatedly pick compact wrong answers on
  non-convex data. A connectivity-aware criterion or shape prior is needed.
  \item \textbf{Imitation recovers moves, not plans.} The RL agent learned to
  fire \code{eigen\_embed} as a conditional move but not the sequence that
  makes it pay off, and its best smoke result depended on one training run.
  \item \textbf{Silent failures bias means.} GMM's singular covariances, a YAML
  float parsed as a string, a missing default argument and git-ignored
  training files each removed rows from the benchmark without stopping it.
  Each missing row changed the means it was dropped from until it was
  root-caused.
  \item \textbf{The graph, not the point count, is what graph methods need.}
  A thousand micro-clusters linked by the points that lie between them carry
  the connectivity signal that makes LMM and spectral clustering accurate,
  at linear cost. Profile before optimising: the first speed-up came from
  the neighbour search, not the model.
  \item \textbf{Edge weights trade cuts for geometry.} Gaussian weights that
  separate close rings and outliers distort the eigenvectors of smooth,
  gap-free manifolds, and plain boundary counts do the opposite. Giving EM
  both bases was the best compromise we found, and an eigengap switch
  between them was not.
  \item \textbf{State the size of the comparison family.} We first reported
  an OOD win of \code{lmm\_mcg} over LMM as significant after Holm correction
  over three comparisons. The candidates were four variants against three
  references, and over those twelve tests no OOD win survives. Correcting
  over the comparisons actually made is part of the result.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Limitations.} Most tasks are small ($n \le 1000$). The big-data
sweep reaches $n = 200{,}000$ on one generator with one seed, and the
large-$n$ graph comparison uses eleven families at $d = 20$. Twenty families give wide
intervals, so many adjacent ranks are ties. Lineage tables come from earlier,
smaller grids run before the validity controls. Algorithms run with fixed
hyperparameters. Many ideas here have
prior art:
\begin{itemize}
  \item algorithm selection~\cite{rice1976algorithm};
  \item meta-learning for clustering, from early work to recent
  systems~\cite{desouto2008ranking,ferrari2015clustering,trederTschechlov2023ml2dac,singh2024clams,bakhtyari2025clustrecnet};
  \item landmarking~\cite{pfahringer2000landmarking}, which our probe
  features are;
  \item stacked generalisation~\cite{wolpert1992stacked};
  \item Laplacian mixture modelling~\cite{korenblum2018laplacian};
  \item exponential-family mixtures~\cite{banerjee2005clustering};
  \item spectral clustering on centroids and anchor
  graphs~\cite{yan2009fast,liu2010large,huang2020ultra}, competitive Hebbian
  topology graphs~\cite{martinetz1994topology,tasdemir2009exploiting},
  locally scaled affinities~\cite{zelnikmanor2004self} and BIRCH's CF
  triples~\cite{zhang1996birch}, which MCG combines.
\end{itemize}
Recent clustering-recommendation systems such as ML2DAC, CLAMS and
ClustRecNet target the same problem as our routers and should be compared
against directly in future work. A limited search found no published
MRI-relaxation clustering method and no prior agent that composes clustering
algorithms from primitive operators learned by imitation, but neither search
was exhaustive. Our contribution is a systematic, re-runnable evaluation of
these ideas against a broad registry, with validity controls, plus the
FMM/LMM/AMM variants, the trajectory-trained agent and the MCG combination.

\section{Conclusion and future work}
Starting from benchmark failure modes, we built 39 algorithms and ten
learned selectors and evaluated all of them against one another, including on
data none had seen. The most durable results are these:
\begin{itemize}
  \item graph-based clustering by connectivity, in the form of LMM, an
  EM-trained mixture over a graph-Laplacian basis;
  \item its linear-cost counterpart MCG, which keeps that accuracy on a graph
  of micro-clusters and is 54 times faster at $n = 100{,}000$;
  \item an explanation of why both work on manifolds: their eigenvectors are
  Fourier modes along the data;
  \item a negative result: learned routers win only on dataset families they
  have seen.
\end{itemize}
Four directions follow:
\begin{itemize}
  \item fine-tune the behaviour-cloned agent with
  PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximal}, rewarding terminal partition quality, using
  the trajectory corpus every benchmark run now produces;
  \item replace specific weak components, such as CLARANS's swap proposal and
  Chameleon's merge rule, with models trained on trajectory data;
  \item give selection gates a shape-aware criterion, e.g.\ penalise
  silhouette when the number of $k$NN-graph components disagrees with $k$;
  \item make MCG's resolution adaptive, so that small $n$ with large $k$
  (Olivetti faces) and tightly packed 2-D structures get enough
  micro-clusters, and replace LMM's $(k, M, n)$ Newton step so that the
  EM-based variants scale in $k$.
\end{itemize}
